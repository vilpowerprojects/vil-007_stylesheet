English Learners

Scaffold to Offer Different Levels of Support

Support children as they read, identify, say, and write words that rhyme in Targeted Instruction on p. 40.

Substantial Support

Give partners index cards with two sets of rhyming words and one set of non-rhyming words written on them. For example, kitten, mitten, snow, go, rat, white. Have partners match the rhyming words.

As children work, circulate and model correct pronunciation. Encourage partners to think of other words that rhyme with the ones on their cards.

Moderate Support

Distribute sentence strips to children containing rhyming and non-rhyming lines of a traditional poem read in class. Have children first identify the rhyming words by highlighting them and then rearrange the strips to match rhyming lines.

Challenge the group to brainstorm as many words as possible that rhyme with each set of identified rhymes.

Light Support

Distribute sentence strips to children containing rhyming and non-rhyming lines of a traditional poem read in class, with blanks where the rhyming words would go. Have children complete the sentences with the correct word and then rearrange the strips to match rhyming lines. Challenge partners to change the non-rhyming lines into silly rhymes. Invite volunteers to share their new rhymes.

Build Words! (40 MIN.)

Phonics

Words with -ck

SUPPORT LANGUAGE TRANSFER

There is no -ck at the end of words in Haitian Creole. Write rock. Underline ck. Say /k/ and then say words with the same ending sound with children.

Teach

Write and display the letters c and k. Remind children that c and k together stand for one sound, /k/. Then write and display the letters i, l, o, and r. Call on children to name their sounds. Use SS&A Cards to reteach the connection between the sounds and letters.

Blend Words Routine

Model

Write and display the word lock.

Say the Sounds: Teacher scripting“I am going to say each sound in the word lock: l stands for /l/, o stands for /ŏ/, and the letters c and k together stand for /k/.”

Blend the Sounds Together: Teacher scripting“Now listen as I blend these sounds together: /lllŏk/, lock. Say the word with me: lock.”

Apply

Write and display the word rock.

Say the Sounds: Teacher scripting“Your turn! Say each sound in the word.” Annotation(/r/ /ŏ/ /k/)

Blend the Sounds Together: Teacher scripting“Now blend the sounds together.” Annotation(/rrrŏk/, rock)

Then use the routine and have children blend sounds to say the words below.

dock        sock        pick        kick

Draw a line under -ck. Have children say /k/, blend the sounds, and say pick. Repeat for the words sock and kick.

Teach

Review the Summarize strategy.

  • Display and revisit the Summarize Anchor Chart. Review the steps to summarize the text.

  • Remind children that the steps to summarize a text are:

    − identify key details, or “important facts or information.”

    − put the details together to explain the most important information in the text in their own words.

  • Remind children that key details help explain the topic of the text. Say, Teacher scripting“Remember that when we summarize, we ‘briefly tell, in our own words, the key details of a text.’ We do not tell every detail from the text. We think carefully about the words and what the most important ideas are.”

The author also gives an example of rhyming words from the traditional rhyme, ‘Hickory Dickory Dock’: dock and clock. These words have the same sounds at the end.”

  • On the graphic organizer, record the following as a key detail: Traditional rhymes have rhyming words.

TIP

In this text and in the unit, there are two definitions of rhyme: rhyme as a feature of texts (as in rhyming lines or sounds) and rhyme as a type of text (as in nursery rhymes). Focus on rhymes as text features (words or lines that sound the same at the end). If children are confused about rhyme as a kind of text, explain that a traditional rhyme is a kind of poem that gets its name because it includes rhyming words.

CROSSLINGUISTIC CONNECTIONS

In Spanish, rhyming words sound the same at the end like they do in English (for example, fresa/mesa in Spanish). In Vietnamese and Mandarin, rhyme may follow different sound patterns, and the pitch of a word can change its meaning. Say, Teacher scripting“Let’s listen for rhyming words in English. If you’ve heard rhymes in your home language, you can tell us what they sound like too.”

Identify Features of Poetry (10 MIN.)

Revisit today’s Learning Goal. Say, Teacher scripting“We will identify rhyme in a poem.”

TEACH Introduce how to identify rhyming words in a poem.

  • Remind children that rhyming words are “words that sound the same at the end.”

    − Say, Teacher scripting“Listen to these words that rhyme: clock, dock. These words have the same sounds at the end.”

  • Display the Once Upon a Rhyme Chant. Explain that the chant is a poem.

  • Read aloud the poem and stress the first two rhyming words: fun, everyone. Explain that fun and everyone rhyme. Both words sound the same at the end.

  • Point out that these rhyming words are at the end of the lines.

TIP

While you read the chant aloud, track print to point out that rhyming words can be within the same line or at the end of lines.

MODEL Model identifying other rhyming words in the poem.

Think Aloud: Teacher scripting“When I listen to the poem, I hear other words that rhyme. I will read the third line: Kittens with mittens and brave little eggs. I hear two words that rhyme, or sound the same at the end. Kittens and mittens rhyme.”
